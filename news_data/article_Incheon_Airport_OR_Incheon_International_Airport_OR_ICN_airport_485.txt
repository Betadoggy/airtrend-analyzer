Unrestricted Warfare

Recently, there was a fire at South Korea’s national data center, which has raised doubts about its relation and importance to national security. Any country must protect itself from spies and foreign agents. When counterintelligence systems break down and when data and records are destroyed, then the nation loses its ability to defend itself. Was that fire simply negligence? I suspect not, and rather see it is a form of warfare. The fire in the U.S. company building Coupang also smacks of arson — a potential way to try and replace this major American company with CCP competitors. When added to other instances of fires at key locations I suspect to be arsons, a pattern emerges. (RELATED: The End Of The Miracle That Is South Korea)

So far, the only element missing from this push toward authoritarianism has been open gunfire. But the nature of warfare is changing. It is no longer simply what experts call kinetic, where physical weapons and encounters dominate the battlefield. We are living through an era of unrestricted hybrid warfare in which attacks occur across information, cyber, psychological, economic and institutional domains.

I can see, along with many others, that it is essential to prevail in this conflict before it escalates into kinetic war. In a plausible worst case scenario, if China were to move against Taiwan while North Korea simultaneously launched an offensive to seize South Korea, the world could face the beginnings of World War Three. However, something even more sinister is happening in front of our very eyes.

An Embedded Threat

For decades, North Korea’s stated goal has been reunification under its own rule. Few would have imagined that South Korea could be communized from within — that elite capture and institutional corrosion could leave the country open to a form of unification on communist terms. Yet that is precisely the danger potentially now confronting the Republic of Korea. It follows a familiar pattern seen elsewhere, such as in places like Venezuela, where a dangerous form of communism has reduced the country to ruin, including the abandonment of the rule of law.

In South Korea, churches are being raided. This has never happened before at such a scale in South Korean history. Religious and civic leaders are being prosecuted, investigated and harassed through lawfare. Even the 92-year-old Reverend Billy Kim — often called the Billy Graham of Korea, retired pastor of one of the world’s largest Baptist congregations and longtime head of the Far East Broadcasting Company — has reportedly faced raids on his offices. Why would a peaceful spiritual leader at his age be treated as a threat? Such actions are deeply troubling to all of us.

What makes these measures appear so dangerous to me is that, while unknown to the vast majority of the population, they are designed to intimidate and silence dissent. When I last visited Korea, I was met by a joyful gathering of patriots and freedom advocates at Incheon Airport — perhaps the largest public welcome ever recorded there. Police investigated roughly six hundred citizens present for alleged public disturbance despite the peaceful nature of the event. At least two were indicted under these suspicious circumstances. Once again, I have to ask: “Do Koreans no longer have the right to gather peacefully for civic or patriotic purposes?”

Democracy Restored

Despite this repression, I believe there is hope. Research has shown that throughout modern history, nearly all non-violent resistance movements have succeeded whenever roughly 3.5 percent of a nation’s population actively participate in such movements.

I see signs that such a successful movement may already be forming in South Korea. To prevail, it must remain unified, disciplined and strategic. Sustained non-violent resistance typically requires about three years to achieve decisive change. I urge Korean patriots to persevere, to stand together rather than against one another, and to remain steadfast until democracy is secured. (RELATED: Sky-High Diplomacy: Japan Refuels South Korean Jets)

When this non-violent strategy is adopted, then boycotts can be one of the effective tactics employed to help bring about success. During the U.S. civil-rights movement of the 1960s, citizens refused to patronize businesses that upheld injustice. Similarly, Koreans might choose to support those institutions and enterprises standing for freedom and withhold support from those advancing repression.

And we must never underestimate the power of words! We can persuade through personal relationships, online commentary or formal petitions to members of the National Assembly. We must always remember that governments cannot suppress an entire population: it is too costly and too dangerous for them. Our hope resides in winning a very simple contest — between perseverance and intimidation — between those who love the country and those whose policies are pushing it down a road to destruction.

Reverse Elite Capture

Another thought that will help in the struggle we face in South Korea is to beat the Chinese at their own game. A well-known strategy employed by the CCP is known as elite capture, co-opting influential figures through bribery, threats or blackmail to steer societies in its favor. One proposal to find a solution to getting out of this impasse that South Korea must pursue is what I call reverse elite capture —elevating honest, patriotic and incorruptible leaders who will act in the nation’s true interest.

Furthermore, the South Korean police must ensure it doesn’t cooperate with any foreign communist forces that may be operating in the country. The military must not allow its defenses to be dismantled or strategic lands to be sold to CCP-linked buyers. Judges, too, must find the courage to uphold the law impartially and resist political pressure.

Lawful Action Against Tyranny

Let us return to the point I made at the beginning of my previous article — the importance of the rule of law. Recently, even the Supreme Court of Korea has come under political assault. Democratic lawmakers have gone after the Court and effectively trampled on the bench, expressing their contempt for it. They have pressured justices, including the Chief Justice himself, to condone and align with the executive branch. In a constitutional republic, such interference is intolerable. The judiciary must remain independent, applying the law without fear or favor, rather than acting like partisan politicians.

There were multiple criminal cases pending against President Lee Jae Myung when he took office in 2025. No president should be above the law. In the United States, even a sitting president is not necessarily immune from criminal prosecution for acts deemed unofficial. The same standard should apply in Korea, where cases should proceed on their merits, without political obstruction.

A less recognized danger to the rule of law lies not in excessive investigation and legal procedure, but in the lack of it. We see this in action in two ways. The Lee administration has tried to commandeer the prosecutor’s office so that it is no longer independent. Courts then refuse to authorize investigations and examine important evidence on the merits. So prosecutors decline to act, and entire institutions are compromised by partisan influence. What Korea needs now are brave judges, principled attorneys and honest officials willing to uphold the rule of law and restore democracy to the people. (RELATED: Why South Korea’s Election Integrity Should Matter To America)

The independent judiciary is the bulwark of any constitutional republic. If judges bow to political power in a lawless way, the very foundation of freedom crumbles. Even one decisive case, say affirming electoral integrity or holding a corrupt leader accountable, could end a tyranny and revive the nation’s democratic spirit.

Ultimately, my hope rests in my belief in God and His purpose. As Koreans wait patiently and act courageously, I am praying that divine justice will prevail. I would like to give a simple message to the people of South Korea: keep faith, keep fighting for justice and keep standing for truth. Korea can once again shine as a beacon of liberty, freedom and justice in the world. May that renewal come soon — and may it inspire democracies everywhere to defend the rule of law before it becomes too late.

Morse Tan is a former U.S. Ambassador-at-Large for Global Criminal Justice.
